Perching mechanisms in drones can allow extended mission times, noise reduction during data collection,
and aid in placing sensors in inaccessible locations. In addition to perching mechanisms, methods of detecting perches
are needed to support autonomy or aid operators in limited line-of-sight.
%
%
This thesis designed a perch grasping and detection mechanism for grasping overhead objects such as tree branches or
powerlines. The grasping device was designed to be simple to fabricate on a laser cutter with wood or plastic, and 
to remain passively attached after perching is complete. The detection mechanism was implemented with stereo vision, 
to make use of low-cost camera sensors and open-sourced image processing tools. 
%
%
The grasping device was verified under controlled, indoor conditions to support drone of at least 500g, accounting for 
dynamic load based on 63.17 $m/s^2$ estimated worst case acceleration. The device was verified to allow repeated
grasping and releasing over at least 100 cycles.
%
%
The proposed detection method showed promise for use in autonomous drone perching, with a sucess rate exceeding 
80\% under optimal conditions and a detection error under 75mm for distances within 2m, and under $8^\circ$
error in orientation measurement. However further work is needed to refine the concept. 
In particular the method used is very sensitive to patterns and colors of the background, which can 
prevent detection of a perch. Additionally, the estimated orientation of a perch becomes more unreliable the closer a perch is,
posing issues for integrating the detection mechanism into a flight controller. Improving the filtering of background 
points which are unlikely to be perches, or are likely to have incorrect depth data, is likely to be the 
most impactful improvement to be made in future work.
