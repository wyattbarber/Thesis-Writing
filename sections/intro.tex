\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Application of Perching Drones}

Much recent research has been dedicated to developing multi-modal drones that can transition between flight
and a stationary mode attached to a structure. Often referred to as "perching", this behavior is intended
to enable the use of drones over longer mission times, where perching can reduce power use, or in scenarios
where the noise of a drones running motors is undesirable. Often the targeted use case for this is ecology research,
where drones could place a sensor payload in an inaccessible forest canopy, or monitor wildlife without the disruptive
noise of its motors. 

Forest canopies support highly varied microclimates, which support diverse ecosystems and may provide additional resilience to climate change.
These systems are poorly understood, primarily due to the difficulty of observation and data collection. Often canopy cranes are used for
forest observation, however these are costly and difficult to use for geographically dense or replicated data. They are also very large,
and can themselves cause disruption to the system being studied.
The variance in habitat of the forest canopy is particularly pronounced in tropical forests, with a study in the Philippines
showing higher variability in temperature and moisture between the forest floor and canopy compared to a change in elevation
10 times greater \cite{nakamura_forests_2017}.

Tropical forests are a primary target for global restoration and carbon sequestration efforts,
so increasing knowledge of these forests is critical for understanding how to protect them and the global climate \cite{mrema_ten_2020}.
Drones have potential as methods of collecting better data about forests. Being mobile, they can collect data over larger areas without the
need to construct replicated observation stations. The density of obstacles in a forest canopy can be a challenge for drones, but methods
to navigate similar environments have been proven \cite{mulgaonkar_robust_2018,tan_design_2017,liu_mini-drone_2023}. Two major problems
remain for using drones in ecological data collection: they are very loud and disruptive to wildlife being studied
\cite{jenni-eiermann_unmanned_2017,bennitt_terrestrial_2019},
and they have a short flight time due to their high power consumption.

Another use case for perching drones is power line inspection. Drones used here can accelerate the inspection process
while reducing the risk that inspectors are exposed to, both from high voltage and high heights.
A drone used in this case could even charge inductively from the power lines it is inspecting \cite{hoang_autonomous_2024}.

\subsection{Modes of Perching}


Perching drones can provide a workaround to these issues. Perching drones are capable of attaching themselves to objects in the environment in
order to maintain a fixed position without consuming power. Perching mechanisms are typically adapted to a structure
that is expected to be found in the environment where the drone operates \cite{hang_perching_2019}.
A review by Meng et al. \cite{meng_aerial_2022} describes three categories of perching mechanism:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Grasping: often inspired by bird claws, this type of mechanism grips a branch and holds a drone
            stationary on top of it. An example is shown in figure \ref{fig:claw}
    \item Embedding: more similar to how insects grasp a surface, these mechanisms use small spikes to grip rough surfaces.
    \item Attaching: grasping and embedding tend to fail on  smooth surfaces. Attaching uses adhesive forces such as electrostatics or vacuums to
            hold a drone to a smooth surface.
\end{itemize}

Passive methods of perching are desirable as they reduce power consumption, reduce computational power needed,
and can be more robust if well designed \cite{nguyen_passively_2019}. However they can introduce their own
complications, often requiring complex behaviors in the drone to form a strong attachment. The design by Nguyen et al.
\cite{nguyen_passively_2019}, shown in figure \ref{fig:tether} requires the flight controller to be adapted to loop a tether around a branch.
This example also requires space to maneuver around the branch the drone is perching on. The mechanism
proposed by Kirchgeorg and Mintchev \cite{kirchgeorg_hedgehog_2022} requires less space to move,
however it still requires adaptations to the flight controls to maintain a smooth lowering of the drone onto its perch.
This design is illustrated in figure \ref{fig:hedgehog}.

Perching mechanisms found in nature are often used as inspiration for designs of perching drones, such as a birds grasping
claws or an insects embedding spikes. Another possible inspiration is the grasping tendrils produced by many
climbing vines. Plant tendrils are able to grasp nearby objects of unknown shape and size to support the
main stem they grow from. They do this by growing out straight and then curling progressively tighter
around objects they make contact with \cite{klimm_force_2023}.
% Maybe just get rid of this?
Such a grasping mechanism could be useful to perching drones, as it would allow them to latch onto branches or other objects in the
vicinity without complex flight controls. The actuator designed by Must et al. \cite{must_variable-stiffness_2019} mimics the grasping behavior
and mechanism of plant tendrils, however it appears to be too slow and delicate for use as a drone grasping mechanism.
